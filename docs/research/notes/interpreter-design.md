beat QuickJS-NG on Svelte-style code without a JIT: dispatch, bytecode shape, values, objects and
inline caches, calls and closures, strings, numbers. The last part is a concrete v1 proposal.

## Summary

**Findings**

1. **No interpreter tier closes the V8 gap, but a good one can double QuickJS.**
   - On the same three Svelte workloads, V8 with every JIT tier off (Ignition only: a register
     and accumulator interpreter with feedback-vector ICs) is **2.3x QuickJS-NG on signals, 1.65x on
     app glue and 1.17x on Svelte proxies** (measured, by a co-researcher; §2.4). Full V8 is ~19x.
   - On Octane, the best interpreters reach 1.6–3.7x QuickJS-NG: V8 jitless, Hermes v1, JSC
     without JIT, Escargot (documented, §2.4).
   - The ceiling for hocus v1 is therefore roughly 2x, not 20x (inferred).
2. **Every Rust JS engine today is far slower than QuickJS.** On Octane:
   - Boa is 0.20–0.33x QuickJS-NG.
   - Brimstone, which is Ignition-inspired with a compacting GC, is 0.16–0.30x.
   - Nova is 0.07–0.11x.

   (documented, javascript-zoo, §2.4.) Rust does not make an interpreter fast. Design and
   engineering of the fast paths do. Most of the risk in this project sits here.
3. **The measured opcode mix says where QuickJS spends its dispatches on Svelte code** (measured, §2.3).
   - **31–41% of executed opcodes only move data:** local and argument get/put, `dup`/`drop`, push
     constant.
   - **17–19% read closure variables** (`get_var_ref`; Svelte's module-level `active_reaction`,
     `untracking` and so on).
   - **Only 8–18% are property accesses.**
   - **Each bytecode call runs 40 opcodes on signals and 14 on app glue.**

   A register VM removes most of the moves. A tracing GC removes the refcount traffic that every
   QuickJS move carries. Cheaper calls matter more than ICs for this code.
4. **Dispatch technique is worth 5–60%, depending on how heavy the opcodes are.**
   - Wasmi 2.0 measures tail-call threading at 1.6x over `loop { match }` on CoreMark
     (documented).
   - CPython 3.14's tail-calling interpreter gives 3–5%, and the 10–15% first reported was a
     clang-19 regression in the baseline (documented).
   - My toy register VM measured parity between the two (measured, §1.6).
   - JS opcodes are heavy, so expect the low end: 5–20% (inferred).
5. **Tail calls in stable Rust work today, without a guarantee.**
   - `become` (`explicit_tail_calls`, rust-lang/rust#112788) is still unstable in late 2026. Its
     project goal aims to stabilize it in 2027 (documented).
   - Stitch and wasmi 2.0 rely on LLVM sibling-call optimisation on stable, with a portable
     loop-match fallback (documented).
   - That works on x86-64 and aarch64, but a toolchain change can silently break it. Rust 1.92 cost
     stitch about 30% on CoreMark (documented).
6. **ICs help, but less than the folklore says on this code, and they must not cost memory for
   run-once code.**
   - QuickJS-NG added a polymorphic IC (PR #120, Octane +2.4%; DeltaBlue +10%, Box2D +19%) and
     removed it in February 2025 (PR #884). On web-tooling it traded memory for CPU: typescript was
     0.89x without it, prettier 1.54x (documented).
   - The fixes are known from V8 and JSC: allocate feedback lazily (V8 7.7: 2–8% of heap saved)
     and keep bytecode immutable, with IC state in a side table (JSC 2019) (documented).

**Recommendation (hocus v1)**

- **Bytecode:** a register machine with an implicit accumulator (Ignition and Sparkplug style). The
  opcode is 1 byte, operands are 1 byte with `Wide`/`ExtraWide` prefixes, and bytecode is
  immutable and serializable. Every IC site carries a feedback-slot operand.
- **Dispatch:** one handler body per opcode, generated by a macro into two modes:
  - indirect-threaded tail calls on x86-64 and aarch64 (stable Rust, with a CI check that every
    handler ends in a `jmp`);
  - `loop { match }` for wasm32 and anything else.

  Move to `become` when it stabilises.
- **Value:** a 64-bit NaN-boxed `Copy` newtype with an int32 fast path and unboxed f64. Its pointer
  payload can be a raw GC pointer or a handle index, whichever the GC study picks.
- **Objects:** hidden classes with transition tables, inline slots sized per allocation site, and
  dictionary mode as a fallback. Elements kinds: packed int32/f64/Value and holey variants.
- **ICs:** monomorphic, then up to 4-way polymorphic, then a global megamorphic cache, all living
  in a lazily allocated per-function feedback vector.
- **Calls:** frames in one contiguous register file. A JS→JS call does not recurse on the Rust
  stack, and arguments are passed by overlapping register windows. Captured variables live in
  heap scope objects, and module variables are direct slots.
- **Expected speed vs QuickJS-NG** (inferred, §8.6):
  - Svelte signals: 1.5–2.0x.
  - App glue: 1.3–1.7x.
  - Proxy-heavy: 1.0–1.3x.
  - wasm32 (no tail calls): about 20% less.

  The risk band for a first cut is 0.7–1.3x, which is where the Rust engines above sit or worse.
  Gate the port on measured parity.

## 1. Dispatch in Rust

### 1.1 What the compiler does with a big `match`

- **The classic problem** (documented, Mike Pall, lua-l, 2011-02-15,
  <http://lua-users.org/lists/lua-l/2011-02/msg00742.html>):
  - one giant control-flow graph with nested diamonds defeats register allocation;
  - slow paths pollute fast paths;
  - one shared indirect jump predicts badly.

  LuaJIT 2's interpreter is hand-written assembly (DynASM `vm_*.dasc`) for that reason.
- **Deegen** (Xu and Kjolstad, arXiv:2411.11469, 2024; documented) generates interpreters at the
  LLVM IR level:
  - one function per bytecode, tail-calling the next;
  - the GHC calling convention, so there are no callee-saved registers;
  - hot/cold splitting;
  - per-bytecode inline caches.

  Its LuaJIT Remake interpreter is **31% faster than LuaJIT's hand-written interpreter and 179%
  faster than PUC Lua** across 44 benchmarks (abstract). The author's blog
  (<https://sillycross.github.io/2022/11/22/2022-11-22/>, documented) credits inline caching as
  "one of the most important" reasons, and explains that `musttail` alone still wastes 6–8 of the 15
  x86-64 GPRs on callee-saved registers.
- **QuickJS-NG** uses GCC/Clang computed goto (`DIRECT_DISPATCH` = 1 except on MSVC and
  Emscripten; `quickjs.c:51-55 @ 7a23d8e`, source-observed). It has a single `JS_CallInternal`
  function with all 252 opcode bodies inlined (`quickjs-opcode.h`: 252 `DEF` plus 19 short-form
  `def`; source-observed). Its 71% self time in the svelters perf profile is this function
  (documented, `svelters/docs/notes/quickjs-jit-cranelift.md` §5).

### 1.2 The options in Rust

| Technique | Stable Rust? | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `loop { match op }` | yes | LLVM emits a jump table; in my toy it did **not** tail-duplicate the dispatch (one indirect `jmp`) (measured, §1.6). Wasmi 1.0, Boa (call-loop), Nova use loops. |
| Call-loop: `loop { table[op](ctx, ...) }` | yes | Boa's dispatch (`core/engine/src/vm/mod.rs:988-1010 @ 64c209f`, source-observed). Wasmi measured it at **-60%** vs direct tail calls (documented, below). |
| Tail-call threading by sibling-call optimisation | yes, not guaranteed | Stitch and Wasmi 2.0 (documented, below). It works when every handler has the same signature and ends in a call in tail position. My toy compiled every handler to `jmp *%rax` (measured). |
| `become` (`explicit_tail_calls`) | **nightly only** | rust-lang/rust#112788, B-experimental, RFC 3407 not accepted. LLVM backend support exists; open items are by-move arguments and target support (documented, issue page read 2026-10-25). |
| `extern "rust-preserve-none"` | nightly only | feature `rust_preserve_none_cc`, tracking #151401 (documented, Unstable Book). This is the Rust spelling of clang's `preserve_none`, which removes the callee-saved register problem Deegen describes. |
| `#[loop_match]` (computed-goto-like) | nightly, experimental | mentioned in the Trifecta project-goal post (documented, below). |

**Status of `become` (late 2026)** (documented):

- **Not stable.** Trifecta's 2026 project goal (Folkert de Vries, 2026-04-14,
  <https://trifectatech.org/blog/tail-calls-project-goal/>) aims to "finish the groundwork in
  2026 and stabilize in 2027", and the goal is still unfunded.
- **Targets that work:** x86, x86-64, aarch64, arm, s390x, RISC-V, LoongArch, wasm32 (with the
  `tail-call` feature, which is "immature and performs poorly for interpreters") and powerpc64le.
- **Targets that don't:** PowerPC's TOC breaks cross-object tail calls.
- **On the tracking issue** (2026-07-12), Robbepop posted Wasmi CoreMark under four dispatch modes,
  with everything else the same:
  - direct tail-call 2820;
  - indirect tail-call 2357 (-17%);
  - loop-match 1764 (-38%);
  - loop-call 1133 (-60%).

  Coastalwhite reported **-7.9% wall time** (branch misses -47%) from swapping a loop for `become`
  plus `rust-preserve-none`.

### 1.3 Real-world Rust interpreters

**Wasmi**

- **v0.32 (2024-05-28)** moved from stack-based to register-based IR. CoreMark rose 1.7–2.2x
  (Epyc 7763: 657 → 1457; M2 Pro: 884 → 1577). Call-heavy recursive fib "did not improve
  significantly" (documented, <https://wasmi-labs.github.io/blog/posts/wasmi-v0.32/>).
- **v2.0 (2026-09-01)** (documented, <https://wasmi-labs.github.io/blog/posts/wasmi-v2.0/>):
  - **Speed:** about **2.2x geomean over 1.0** (M2 Pro).
  - **Dispatch:** direct threading by default (function pointers embedded in the IR, tail-calling
    the next handler). Indirect threading (opcode plus jump table) is "~10–15% slower" but uses
    "significantly less IR memory". The switch-loop "leaves a lot of performance on the table,
    especially on Apple Silicon".
  - **Handlers:** each takes 9 arguments (`store, ip, sp, mem0, mem0_len, instance, ireg, freg32,
    freg64`). Two of them, `ireg`/`freg*`, are **accumulators held in machine registers**. They
    cost extra copy instructions, mitigated by fused `op + local.set` instructions.
  - **Other choices:** separate value and call stacks; 64-bit cells (128-bit cells had cost 5–10%).
  - **The 2.2x includes one compiler fix.** Rust 1.92's `DestinationPropagation` merged two
    dispatch sites into one indirect branch. Fixing it (PR #2027) took Wasmi from about 2800 to over
    4200 CoreMark, and stitch fell about 30% on the same toolchain change. The lesson they draw:
    inspect the assembly (`cargo-show-asm`) on every toolchain bump.
  - **Platform coverage:** `auto-dispatch` selects tail-call dispatch only where it is known to work
    and is "very conservative" (author on HN, documented).

**Stitch** (makepad/stitch, documented, README):

- A wasm interpreter that relies on sibling-call optimisation, and states that LLVM "does not
  strictly guarantee" it.
- It does not run on 32-bit targets.
- CoreMark (M2 Pro): stitch 2950, wasm3 2911, wasmi (pre-2.0) 788, wasmtime 12807.

**Boa** (source-observed, `@ 64c209f`):

- **Dispatch:** a call-loop over `OPCODE_HANDLERS[opcode](context, pc)`. Every iteration re-reads
  `self.vm.frame().code_block.bytecode.bytes.get(pc)` through the frame stack, with a bounds check
  (`vm/mod.rs:988-1010`).
- **Bytecode:** register operands (`opcode/mod.rs:235`). Boa v0.21 moved from stack to registers
  (documented, release notes).
- **ICs:** a polymorphic IC with `PIC_CAPACITY = 4` and weak shapes (`vm/inline_cache/mod.rs:16`).
- **Values:** NaN-boxed by default (`value/inner.rs`; `jsvalue-enum` is the legacy feature).
- **Speed:** despite all of that, 0.20–0.33x QuickJS-NG on Octane (§2.4). My inference is that the
  remaining costs are structural:
  - `&mut Context` passed to every handler, with frame state reloaded from memory per op;
  - `GcRefCell` borrow flags on object access;
  - spec-literal slow paths.

  This is a hypothesis; I did not profile Boa.

**Nova** (source-observed, `@ 877ef16`):

- `Vm { ip, stack: Vec<Value>, reference_stack, iterator_stack, ..., result: Option<Value> }`
  (`engine/bytecode/vm.rs:86`), with a `while let Some(instr)` + `match instr.kind` loop
  (`vm.rs:303`, `vm.rs:463`).
- It is a stack machine with a result register, and spec references live on their own stack.
- The property cache is keyed by property key, not by bytecode site
  (`builtins/ordinary/caches.rs:445`).
- 0.07–0.11x QuickJS-NG on Octane (§2.4).

**CPython 3.14's tail-calling interpreter** (documented):

- **Claim** (What's New, <https://docs.python.org/3.14/whatsnew/3.14.html>): "3–5% faster"
  geomean on pyperformance vs clang-19 without it. It is opt-in, clang ≥ 19, x86-64/AArch64 only.
- **Background** (Nelson Elhage, 2025-03-09, <https://blog.nelhage.com/post/cpython-tail-call/>):
  - The first claim of 10–15% was inflated by an LLVM 19 regression. Limits on tail duplication
    collapsed the computed-goto dispatch from 332 indirect jumps to 3, which made clang-19 builds
    1.09–1.12x slower.
  - Against clang-18 the tail-call gain is "1–5% or so".
  - A switch interpreter without computed goto was only about 2% slower than clang-18.

  The lesson: a dispatch technique that depends on the optimiser can be lost on a compiler bump,
  in either direction.

### 1.4 Direct vs indirect threading, for JS

- Direct threading puts an 8-byte handler pointer in each instruction. That makes JS bytecode much
  larger and not serializable.
- JSC moved from direct to indirect threading in 2019 for memory and cacheability. The change was
  neutral in its C++ CLoop. The new metadata-table indirection cost "a 10% interpreter slowdown" but
  was neutral with the JITs on (documented,
  <https://webkit.org/blog/9329/a-new-bytecode-format-for-javascriptcore/>, 2019-06-21).
- Wasmi puts indirect threading at 10–15% slower than direct (documented).
- svelters wants a bytecode cache (`QUICKJS-RUNTIME.md`). So **hocus should use indirect threading**
  (an opcode byte indexes a static handler table) and give up the 10–15% (inferred).

### 1.5 What safe or mostly-safe Rust can achieve

- **Safe Rust costs bounds checks.** A `loop { match }` over `&[u8]` bytecode, with `Vec<Value>`
  registers, pays a bounds check per register operand and per fetch. These checks predict
  perfectly, so the cost is mostly code size and register pressure. Boa and Nova are written this
  way and are slow, but slow for many reasons (inferred).
- **Fast Rust interpreters use an audited `unsafe` core:**
  - Stitch and Wasmi pass raw `ip`/`sp` between handlers (documented: Wasmi 2.0 handler
    signature).
  - The pattern is a bytecode **verifier** at load time (register indices < frame size, jump
    targets in range, constant indices valid), followed by unchecked access inside one small module
    (inferred).
- **Recommendation:**
  - Keep `unsafe` to the dispatch core (ip, register-file pointer, accumulator), the `Value`
    decoder and the GC.
  - Handlers' slow paths call safe Rust.
  - Run Miri on the portable `match` mode and fuzz the verifier (inferred).

### 1.6 Toy microbenchmark (measured; cloud, not a gate)

**Setup**

- Code: `scratchpad/bench/dispatch/src/main.rs`.
- A register VM running one 6-instruction loop body (add, mov, xor, addi, sub, jnz), implemented
  three ways:
  - `loop { match }` on a Rust enum;
  - a call-loop through a handler table;
  - direct-threaded handlers that tail-call the next one through a function pointer in the
    instruction.
- Build: `CARGO_TARGET_DIR=$PWD/target cargo build --release` (rustc 1.97.0, LTO fat, 1 CGU).
- Run: `./target/release/dispatch 100000000`, 2 runs × 3 rounds.
- Checked with `objdump -d`:
  - every tail handler compiled to a single `jmp *%rax` (e.g. `t_add`: 11 instructions ending in
    `jmp *%rax`);
  - the match loop has exactly one indirect `jmp`.

**Results**

| Variant | ns per VM op (6 rounds) |
| --- | --- |
| `loop { match }` | 1.44–1.62 |
| call-loop | 1.82–2.43 |
| tail-call threaded | 1.66–1.77 |

**Reading:**

- The tail calls compile as intended on stable 1.97.
- On a tiny, perfectly predictable loop, the match is as fast or faster. The test is dominated by
  the register-array store-to-load chain, about 4 cycles per op, and modern indirect predictors
  handle one dispatch site well.
- The call-loop is the slowest, as in Wasmi's numbers.
- This toy cannot show the register-pressure benefit of tail calls, which appears with large,
  branchy handlers. Rely on the published Wasmi numbers for that.

## 2. Stack vs register bytecode

### 2.1 Published evidence

- **Shi, Gregg, Beatty, Ertl, "Virtual Machine Showdown: Stack Versus Registers"** (VEE 2005,
  <https://usenix.org/legacy/events/vee05/full_papers/p153-yunhe.pdf>; documented). Translating
  JVM stack code to register code removed **47% of executed VM instructions**. The bytecode grew
  **25%**, and run time fell **32.3% with switch dispatch and 26.5% with threaded dispatch**
  (Pentium 4). The 2008 TACO version gives 46% and 26%.
- **Wasmi v0.32:** stack to register gave 1.7–2.2x on CoreMark, with no gain on call-heavy fib
  (documented, §1.3).
- **Engines** (documented, their own docs and the zoo classification):
  - register-based: Lua 5.0 (Ierusalimschy et al., "The Implementation of Lua 5.0", 2005), Hermes,
    JSC, Escargot, Brimstone;
  - register plus accumulator: V8 Ignition;
  - stack-based: QuickJS, PrimJS, SpiderMonkey, XS, MuJS.
- **Ignition's accumulator** is "an implicit input and output register for many bytecodes". It
  shrinks bytecode, which is "between 50% to 25% the size of the equivalent baseline machine code",
  and saved about 5% of memory per tab (documented, <https://v8.dev/blog/ignition-interpreter>,
  2016-08-23).
- **JSC's format:** narrow instructions have 1-byte operands; any overflow makes the whole
  instruction wide with an `op_wide` prefix. Mutable IC data lives in a separate metadata table.
  Bytecode memory fell about 50%, and total memory about 10% on Facebook and Reddit (documented,
  WebKit blog 2019).

### 2.2 How QuickJS's stack code costs more than the dispatch

QuickJS values carry a refcount (source-observed, `@ 7a23d8e`):

- **Every push of a heap value increments a count.** `get_loc`, `get_arg`, `get_var_ref` and `dup`
  all do this through `js_dup` (`quickjs.c:1660`), which writes `JS_REF_COUNT(p)++` to the
  allocator header.
- **Every pop of one decrements it.** `drop`, `put_loc`, and the consuming operand of an opcode do
  this through `JS_FreeValue`, which branches to a free path.
- `get_field` does `JS_FreeValue(ctx, sp[-1])` on the receiver after the lookup (`quickjs.c:19602`).
- **Return frees every local and stack slot** in a loop and closes var_refs (`quickjs.c:20972-20981`).

In a register VM with a tracing GC, `Value` is `Copy`. Reading a register writes nothing and frees
nothing. So the move opcodes disappear, and the hidden refcount work in the ones that remain
disappears too (inferred). The svelters perf profile shows `JS_FreeValue` at 3% self time on top of
what is inlined into `JS_CallInternal` (documented).

### 2.3 Measured opcode mix of QuickJS-NG on Svelte workloads

Method (measured):

- I copied QuickJS-NG `7a23d8e` into `scratchpad/opmix/qjs-src` and added a counter to the
  dispatch macro (`hocus_opc[opcode]++`), plus counters for `JS_CallInternal` entries and
  bytecode-function entries.
- Built with CMake Release (GCC), target `qjs_exe`.
- Ran the three workloads of the earlier JIT study, rebuilt by a co-researcher as one bundle of the
  real Svelte 5.57.2 client runtime (`scratchpad/opmix/w/bundle.js`, sha256 `4a4aea78ca4b7c9b…`):
  `HOCUS_OPMIX=<out> qjs bundle.js <signals|proxy|appmix>`.
- A whole run is 640 warmup + 160 timed calls. Categories come from `scratchpad/opmix/cat.py`.

| Category (share of executed opcodes) | signals | proxy | appmix |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| local/arg get/put/set | 24.1% | 19.5% | 25.8% |
| closure var reads/writes (`get_var_ref*`) | 17.1% | 18.6% | 6.3% |
| arithmetic / compare | 15.5% | 17.4% | 18.5% |
| branches | 14.8% | 19.7% | 3.2% |
| property access (`get_field*`, `put_field`, `get_array_el*`, …) | 8.8% | 8.1% | 17.5% |
| stack shuffle (`dup`, `drop`, `nip`, `swap`, …) | 6.5% | 6.3% | 6.3% |
| push constant / `this` | 6.3% | 5.4% | 9.2% |
| call / return | 6.0% | 3.6% | 10.1% |
| total executed opcodes | 269.0 M | 873.2 M | 101.6 M |
| `JS_CallInternal` entries / bytecode-function entries | 9.30 M / 6.64 M | 24.3 M / 12.5 M | 7.09 M / 7.04 M |
| **opcodes per bytecode call** | **40.5** | **70.1** | **14.4** |

What it says (inferred from the table and the source):

- **Pure data movement (local/arg moves, shuffles, constant pushes) is 31–41% of all dispatches.**
  A register-operand ISA folds most of it into the consuming instruction. It also folds constants
  into immediates.
- **Closure-variable reads are the single hottest opcode on signals and proxy** (`get_var_ref`:
  8.4% and 12.5%). They are the Svelte runtime's module-level mutable state (`active_reaction`,
  `active_effect`, `untracking`, `new_deps`, `skipped_deps`, `read_version`; see `get()` in
  `runtime.js`).
  - In QuickJS each one is `*var_refs[idx]->pvalue` plus `js_dup` (`quickjs.c:18996-19004`).
  - A design with module variables as direct slots makes them one or two loads, with no refcount.
- **Flag tests are common.** `(x.f & DIRTY) !== 0` compiles to about 6 opcodes: `get_field`, push,
  `and`, `push_0`, `strict_neq`, `if_false`. That accounts for the high `and`, `strict_neq`,
  `push_0` and `if_false8` counts on signals.
- **Calls are tiny:** 40 opcodes per bytecode call on signals, 14 on app glue. On signals, 2.7 M of
  the 9.3 M call entries go to native functions (calls minus bytecode calls), for example
  `Array.prototype.push` and `Set.prototype.has`.
- **Rough per-op cost:** stock qjs runs signals at about 1.7–2.7 ms per call of `f()` (co-researcher
  results, §2.4), and each `f()` executes about 336k opcodes (269 M / 800). That is roughly 5–8 ns,
  or 15–22 cycles, per opcode. Most of it is opcode bodies (refcounts, lookups, frames), not
  dispatch. Faster dispatch alone cannot deliver a 2x.

Estimated reduction in dispatches for a register+accumulator ISA on this mix: **25–35%** for
signals. That comes from removing about 60–70% of the move, shuffle and push class, plus 5–8% from
fused compare-and-branch (inferred). That is below Shi et al.'s 47%, because the accumulator design
keeps some `Ldar`/`Star` moves.

### 2.4 How fast an interpreter-only engine can be on this code

**Same Svelte workloads, interpreter tiers** (measured by a co-researcher in this study, raw data
`scratchpad/jit/results.txt`, harness `scratchpad/jit/run.sh`):

- Versions: qjs = QuickJS-NG `7a23d8e` Release; node v22.22.0; bun 1.4.4.
- 5 interleaved rounds were present at 18:53 UTC. The medians and ratios below are mine. qjs varied
  from 16.9 to 30.8 ms per 10 calls across rounds, so read the ratios to ±30%.

| Engine / tier (ms per 10 calls, median of 5) | signals | appmix | proxy |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| QuickJS-NG (stock) | 26.9 | 8.86 | 56.1 |
| V8 Ignition only (`--no-opt --no-maglev --no-sparkplug`) | 11.8 (**2.27x**) | 5.38 (**1.65x**) | 48.0 (**1.17x**) |
| V8 `--jitless` | 13.1 (2.05x) | 5.39 (1.64x) | 47.1 (1.19x) |

### 3.2 Choice for hocus

- **Not a 16-byte enum.** It doubles the register file, property slots and array elements against
  an 8-byte box. QuickJS already pays that (inferred). A Rust `enum` with data is 16 bytes unless
  hand-packed like Nova's, and Nova's packing still allocates non-small doubles on the heap
  (source-observed).
- **A NaN-box, `#[repr(transparent)] struct Value(u64)`, `Copy`.** Decoding goes through
  `#[inline(always)]` methods and `fn kind(self) -> ValueKind<'gc>`, an ordinary Rust enum for
  builtins to `match` on. LLVM folds it away after inlining (inferred). Rust idioms survive at the
  builtin layer, and the hot loop sees a `u64`.
- **Pointer-favouring or double-favouring.** Svelte runtime code is dominated by objects, int32
  flags and small integers (§2.3). JSC's pointer-favouring box makes an object dereference free (no
  mask) and costs one integer add per double decode. SpiderMonkey, Hermes and Boa choose the
  opposite. Recommendation: pointer-favouring (inferred). It is a cheap decision to revisit behind
  the newtype.
- **Handles in a NaN-box.** The payload has at least 48 bits, which is enough for:
  - a raw 48-bit pointer (non-moving GC, or a moving GC with precise roots);
  - or a handle: a 4-bit heap-kind plus a 32-bit arena index, with room for generation bits (Nova
    stores u32 indices; source-observed).

  A handle costs one extra address computation per dereference (`arena_base[kind] + idx *
  stride`), plus a bounds check if done in safe Rust (inferred). The interpreter register file is a
  precise root set either way, so both work. The GC researcher's choice decides; this design holds
  for both.
- **Safety.** Values stored in the register file and in heap slots carry no Rust lifetime. Rust code
  outside the core sees `Local<'scope>`-style branded wrappers (as Nova binds handles to a
  `GcScope`, source-observed, `value.rs:72-90`). Tail-call threading helps here: between two
  opcodes no Rust stack frame holds a `Value`, except the accumulator, which handlers spill to the
  frame before any call that can allocate (inferred).

## 4. Object model and inline caches

### 4.1 QuickJS today (source-observed, `@ 7a23d8e`)

- **Shapes are shared.**
  - Each object has a `JSShape *shape` holding the prototype and an ordered property table.
  - Each shape keeps its own small hash table (`prop_hash_mask`, `hash_table[]` before the
    property array; `JSShape`, `quickjs.c:1108-1121`).
  - Shapes with the same prototype and property sequence are found through a runtime-wide hash
    table (`rt->shape_hash`, initial 64 buckets, `quickjs.c:5597-5660`; `find_hashed_shape_prop`,
    `quickjs.c:6058`).

  This is a hidden-class transition tree implemented as a global hash, not as per-shape transition
  links.
- **Property values are always out of line.** They live in `JSProperty *prop`, a separate
  allocation of 16-byte unions (value, getter/setter pair, var_ref, or autoinit)
  (`quickjs.c:1081-1097`, `JSObject` from `quickjs.c:1125`). There are no in-object slots.
  `JS_PROP_INITIAL_SIZE` is 2 (`quickjs.c:1099`).
- **Lookup per access.** `find_own_property` (`quickjs.c:6836-6858`) hashes the atom, then walks
  the bucket chain comparing atoms. `OP_get_field` (`quickjs.c:19559-19604`) repeats this up the
  prototype chain, takes the slow path for accessors and exotic objects, then `js_dup`s the result.
- **Arrays.** A `fast_array` bit uses `u.array.u.values`, a dense array of 16-byte `JSValue`. There
  are no int or double element kinds.
- **No inline caches since February 2025** (§4.3).

### 4.2 What the fast engines do (documented)

- **Hidden classes with transitions.**
  - V8 maps and JSC structures. Tutorial: Bynens and Meurer, "JavaScript engine fundamentals:
    Shapes and Inline Caches", <https://mathiasbynens.be/notes/shapes-ics>, 2018.
  - Objects keep values in **in-object slots**, with overflow in a separate property store.
  - **Dictionary mode** is used for objects that delete properties or grow large.
- **Elements kinds** (<https://v8.dev/blog/elements-kinds>, 2017). V8 tracks packed and holey
  variants of SMI, DOUBLE and generic elements. Transitions go one way only (SMI → DOUBLE →
  generic, packed → holey), so a numeric array stores raw doubles without boxing.
- **ICs in interpreters:**
  - **V8 Ignition:** each property and call site has a slot in a per-function **feedback vector**,
    which Ignition's IC handlers read and write and the optimizing tiers consume. Since V8 7.7,
    vectors are allocated **lazily**, "only after the function has executed a certain amount of
    bytecode". That saves 2–8% of heap in the lab and 1–6% for real users, with no regressions
    (documented, <https://v8.dev/blog/v8-release-77>).
  - **SpiderMonkey Baseline Interpreter** (Jan de Mooij, 2019-08-30,
    <https://hacks.mozilla.org/2019/08/the-baseline-interpreter-a-faster-js-interpreter-in-firefox-70/>):
    - a generated threaded interpreter that uses the **same IC stubs and frame layout as the
      Baseline JIT**;
    - IC data lives in a `JitScript` created "after a few calls/iterations" (threshold 10);
    - Speedometer went **31 → 52** over the C++ interpreter (Baseline JIT: 69), and Google Docs
      load went 901 → 676 ms.
  - **JSC LLInt:** `get_by_id` caches the structure ID and slot offset. Since 2019 they live in a
    per-CodeBlock metadata table, not in the instruction stream, so bytecode stays read-only and
    cacheable (documented, WebKit blog 2019, §2.1).
  - **Hermes:** one `PropertyCacheEntry { WeakRoot<HiddenClass> clazz; SlotIndex slot; }` per
    site, a monomorphic cache checked as `cacheEntry->clazz == clazzPtr` (source-observed,
    `PropertyCache.h`, `Interpreter.cpp:2256 @ 393a539`).
  - **Deegen/LJR:** one IC entry per bytecode in the interpreter, more in the JIT (documented).
- **Boa:** shared and unique shapes (`object/shape/`) and a 4-entry PIC with weak shape references
  (source-observed).

### 4.3 QuickJS-NG's inline caches, and why they were removed (documented)

- **Added: PR #120, "Add Poly IC"** (littledivy, merged 2023-11-29), condensed from
  openwebf/quickjs.
  - Octane went 3268 → 3347 (+2.4%): DeltaBlue +10.1%, Gameboy +10.5%, Box2D +19.2%, RayTrace
    +4.5%, Typescript +5.6%, RegExp +1.4%.
  - The IC rewrote opcodes in place, which broke `JS_READ_OBJ_ROM_DATA` (issue #206). That flag was
    removed in PR #216.
- **Issue #876, "NG is slower compared to Bellard version"** (opened 2025-02-02):
  - Valgrind showed NG at about 3% more instructions and 2% more peak memory.
  - bnoordhuis called the IC "pure overhead" for code that runs once. Disabling it on
    web-tooling-benchmark gave a mean of 1.037: babel-minify 1.42x, prettier 1.54x, but typescript
    0.89x. Limiting slots to 3 slowed typescript 46%.
  - His question was "We're trading memory for CPU. Which is more important?"
  - A prototype IC (PR #551) regressed some benchmarks.
- **Removed: PR #884** (merged 2025-02-06). A later bisect put the IC's cost at about 3% of memory.
  Most of the instruction regression came from column-number tracking instead.

**Lessons for hocus** (inferred from the above):

1. Allocate IC state lazily, after a function has run a few times (V8 and SpiderMonkey thresholds).
2. Never patch bytecode. Keep IC state in a side table, so bytecode is shareable, cacheable and
   ROM-able.
3. Make the IC cheap enough that a miss costs barely more than the slow path. NG's IC sometimes made
   things slower.
4. Size the polymorphism: 4 entries, then megamorphic. Typescript needed more than 3.

### 4.4 Expected gain from ICs on Svelte code (inferred)

**Signals.**

- Property opcodes are 8.8% of dispatches (§2.3), and `find_own_property` is 7.8% of time (svelters
  note §5).
- Svelte signal objects are object literals with fixed property sets: `source()` builds
  `{f, v, reactions, equals, rv, wv}`, and `derived()` builds an 11-property literal (Svelte
  5.57.2, `reactivity/sources.js:74-81`, `deriveds.js:81-93`).
- `get(signal)` sees both, so its `signal.f` site is 2-way polymorphic: `f` is slot 0 in a source
  and slot 4 in a derived.
- With inline slots and a hit in a 4-way PIC, the hash probe becomes a shape compare plus one load
  from the object's own cache line. That saves most of the 7.8%, plus the `prop` indirection.
- Estimate: **1.05–1.10x from ICs and inline slots alone.**

**App glue.** Property opcodes are 17.5% of dispatches and `find_own_property` is 10.8% of time
(svelters note §5). Estimate: **1.10–1.15x.**

**Proxy.** Every `p[i].done` goes through Svelte's `get` trap, a JS function. Estimate:
**about 1.0x** from ICs.

**Overall.** This matches NG's own +2.4% to +19% range. ICs are needed (method calls, the
prototype chain, and a later JIT), but **on this code they are a 5–15% item. The larger wins are op
count, refcount removal and calls.**

## 5. Calls and closures

### 5.1 QuickJS's call path (source-observed, `@ 7a23d8e`)

**Recursion.** Every JS→JS call (`OP_call*`, `quickjs.c:18581-18598`) recurses into
`JS_CallInternal` on the C stack.

**Prologue** (`quickjs.c:18091-18185`):

1. `js_poll_interrupts`.
2. A tag check and a `class_id` check (non-bytecode functions dispatch through
   `rt->class_array[id].call`).
3. A stack-overflow check, then one `alloca` for args, vars, operand stack and var_ref pointers.
4. Copying args into a new buffer when `argc < arg_count`.
5. A loop writing `JS_UNDEFINED` into every local, and another writing NULL into every var_ref slot.
6. Frame linking.

**Return.** It closes var_refs if any, then `JS_FreeValue`s every local and stack slot
(`quickjs.c:20972-20981`). The caller then frees the callee and argument values
(`quickjs.c:18594-18596`).

**Cost.** With 40 opcodes per call (signals) and 14 (app glue), this fixed cost is a large share of
`JS_CallInternal`'s 71% (inferred).

### 5.2 What cheaper call paths look like (documented)

- **Lua 5.x** keeps arguments in consecutive registers at the top of the caller's frame, and they
  become the callee's first registers. A Lua→Lua call reuses the same `luaV_execute` invocation
  instead of recursing (Lua 5.0 paper; `lvm.c`).
- **V8 Ignition** keeps registers in the machine stack frame together with the bytecode array and
  offset (v8.dev/blog/sparkplug, 2021-05-27). Sparkplug "intentionally creates and maintains a
  frame layout which matches the interpreter's frame", which makes OSR and deopt trivial.
- **Wasmi 2.0** keeps separate value and call stacks, and call handlers encode the callee directly,
  so "calls perform zero lookups" (documented).

### 5.3 hocus call design (inferred, from the above)

- **Register file.** One contiguous `Box<[Value]>` per agent, growable by chunk; frames are windows
  into it.
- **Frame header** in reserved slots below the window: return ip, caller frame base, callee
  function, `this`, `new.target`, argc, and a pointer to the function's feedback vector, or null
  before warm-up.
- **Arguments.** The caller evaluates them into its last registers, which become the callee's
  parameter registers: zero copies and zero allocation. Only the missing declared parameters are
  filled with `undefined`. Locals are cleared with a block fill, which the GC needs because the file
  is scanned.
- **No Rust recursion for JS→JS calls.** The `Call` handler pushes a frame and dispatches the
  callee's first instruction. `Return` pops it and dispatches the caller's next instruction. Rust
  recursion happens only for native→JS re-entry (getters, `Array.prototype.map` callbacks, proxy
  traps), which starts a nested dispatch loop with its own exit marker.
- **Call ICs.** The feedback slot records the callee.
  - If it was a bytecode function last time, check identity and jump to its entry.
  - If it was a known builtin, call its Rust function directly with `&[Value]` borrowed from the
    register file.
  - This covers the 2.7 M native calls on signals (`push`, `has`, `includes.call`).
- **Closures.** Captured variables live in heap scope objects (V8 "contexts"), created only for
  scopes that have a captured binding. The compiler knows which bindings escape. Non-captured
  locals stay in registers.
  - Access is `LdaScopeSlot depth, idx`; depth 0 is one load from the frame's scope register.
  - Module-level bindings, Svelte's hottest reads, are slots of the module scope, reachable in two
    loads with no refcount.
  - QuickJS instead uses one `JSVarRef` per captured variable, pointing into the stack while the
    frame is live and into its own `value` after closing (`JSVarRef`, `quickjs.c:469-497`).
    Closing walks var_refs at every return (`close_var_refs`).
- **Generators and async.** A suspended frame is copied out of the register file into a heap
  object and copied back on resume. QuickJS does the same with `JSAsyncFunctionState` and
  `sf->cur_sp` (source-observed, `quickjs.c:18093-18113`).
- **Exceptions.** A zero-cost exception table keyed by ip. Handlers return a `Throw` exit and the
  unwinder walks frames. No per-op checks beyond a handler's own result test.
- **Proxy traps.** `GetNamedProperty` on a Proxy whose `get` trap is a bytecode function pushes a
  trap frame directly, with a continuation frame kind that runs the spec's invariant checks after
  return. The checks are skipped when the target property is configurable and writable, which is
  the common case for Svelte's state objects. That saves one Rust re-entry per trap. V8 shows how
  little this buys overall, about 1.2x for the interpreter tier on the proxy workload (§2.4). Svelte
  proxy cost is mostly the trap's own JS and `Map` lookups (`map_find_record` 2.4% in the svelters
  profile). Further gains belong in svelters' compiler output, not the engine (inferred).

## 6. Strings

- **QuickJS-NG** (source-observed, `@ 7a23d8e`):
  - `JSString` is 8-bit (Latin-1) or 16-bit (UTF-16) by `is_wide_char`, with a 31-bit length and a
    28-bit hash. Atoms are strings with an `atom_type` (`quickjs.c:696-708`).
  - NG HEAD also has **slices** (`JSStringSlice`) and **ropes** (`JSStringRope` with `left`/`right`
    and a `depth` field; `JS_TAG_STRING_ROPE`) (`quickjs.c:710-722`, `quickjs.h:185`).
  - Property keys are `JSAtom` u32 ids.
- **Boa:** `JsString` is "A Latin1 or UTF-16 encoded, reference counted, immutable string", with
  sequence, slice and static kinds (`core/string/src/lib.rs:1`, `vtable/`; source-observed). No
  ropes found.
- **Nova:** strings are **WTF-8** on the heap, with "WTF-16 indexing … implemented through an index
  mapping". Strings up to 7 bytes live inline in the `Value` (`value.rs:109-120`; source-observed;
  the `wtf8` crate).
- **What Rust interop and parley want:** `&str`, which is UTF-8.
  - **WTF-8** gives zero-copy `&str` for any string without lone surrogates. It makes JS's UTF-16
    semantics (`length`, `charCodeAt`, `slice` by code unit) O(n) without an index, so Nova keeps an
    index mapping.
  - **Latin-1/UTF-16** is cheap for JS semantics. ASCII Latin-1 strings are already valid UTF-8
    (zero-copy `&str`). Non-ASCII Latin-1 and UTF-16 need conversion.
- **Recommendation for hocus** (inferred):
  - Keep QuickJS's Latin-1/UTF-16 dual representation, which suits JS semantics and is what the
    port starts from, and add an **`is_ascii` bit** computed at creation. ASCII strings hand out
    `&str` with zero copy, and UI strings are mostly ASCII.
  - Give other strings a lazily created, cached UTF-8 copy, freed with the string, for the presto
    text path.
  - Keep ropes for `+=` and template building, flattened on first index or hand-off to Rust.
  - Keep slices for `substring`.
  - Keep u32 atoms for property keys, so a shape lookup compares integers.
  - Short-string inlining in the `Value` (Nova) saves allocations, but it gives up atom identity for
    keys. Skip it in v1.

## 7. Numbers

- **QuickJS** has int32 (`JS_TAG_INT`) and f64 (`JS_TAG_FLOAT64`) inline, plus inline short BigInts
  (`JS_TAG_SHORT_BIG_INT`) next to heap BigInts (source-observed, `quickjs.h:178-199`). Its opcode
  bodies have int32 fast paths.
- **V8** has 31-bit Smis and boxes other numbers as heap numbers (documented). **JSC, Hermes, Boa
  and SpiderMonkey** keep an int32 tag inside the NaN-box and store f64 unboxed (source-observed).
- **Svelte's runtime is int32-heavy:** flags (`f & DIRTY`), versions (`rv`, `wv`, `read_version`)
  and indices. `and` alone is 3.6% of signals dispatches (§2.3).
- **hocus:**
  - int32 tag in the NaN-box;
  - arithmetic handlers test "both int32" with one mask-and-compare on the two tags, then
    `checked_add`/`checked_sub`/`checked_mul` (compiling to `jo`), and fall back to f64;
  - bitwise ops convert f64 with `ToInt32` only on the slow path.
- **BigInt:** inline i32 short BigInts under a dedicated NaN-box tag, as QuickJS does, and
  heap-allocated arbitrary precision otherwise (another note covers the crate choice). BigInt is
  cold for Svelte code (inferred).

## 8. Proposal: hocus v1 interpreter

### 8.1 Bytecode

- **Register machine plus implicit accumulator** (Ignition-style). Most opcodes take 0–2 register
  operands and read or write the accumulator. Why:
  1. **Bytecode size.** Ignition's case (§2.1) matters for a GUI that caches bytecode for whole
     apps.
  2. **A natural fit for tail-call handlers.** The accumulator is a handler argument, so it stays
     in a machine register across dispatches, as Wasmi 2.0's `ireg`/`freg` do (documented, §1.3).
  3. **One-to-one with a Sparkplug-style baseline JIT** that keeps it in a fixed register.
  4. **Proven on exactly this workload:** V8 Ignition is 2.3x QuickJS-NG on signals (§2.4).
- **Encoding.**
  - 1-byte opcode and 1-byte operands.
  - `Wide` (u16) and `ExtraWide` (u32) prefixes cover the whole instruction (JSC narrow/wide, V8
    prefix scaling).
  - Relative i8/i16/i32 jumps.
  - Constant-pool indices for atoms and literals.
- **Feedback slots.** Every property, call, binary-op and compare site carries a u8/u16 slot index
  into the function's feedback vector.
- **Immutable** after compilation: position-independent, serializable for svelters' bytecode cache,
  and checked by a load-time verifier.
- **Superinstructions**, chosen from measured opmixes and not guessed. First candidates from §2.3:
  - `TestBitsJumpIfZero reg|acc, imm32, off` for `(x.f & K) !== 0` (6 QuickJS ops become 2 with
    the load);
  - `LdaModuleSlot idx`;
  - `GetNamedPropertyFromReg r, atom, slot`;
  - `CallProperty1 r_obj, atom, r_arg, slot` for `deps.push(x)`.

### 8.2 Dispatch

- **One body per opcode.** A `handlers!` macro generates two modes from one source:
  - `mode = "threaded"` (default on x86-64 and aarch64): each handler is
    `fn(ip: *const u8, fp: *mut Value, acc: Value, cx: &mut Cx) -> Exit` and ends with
    `return HANDLERS[*ip as usize](ip, fp, acc, cx)`. That is indirect threading through a static
    table, relying on sibling-call optimisation exactly as stitch and Wasmi do.
  - `mode = "portable"` (wasm32, other targets, and Miri): `loop { match op { … } }` over the same
    bodies.
- **Out-of-line slow paths.** They are `#[cold] #[inline(never)]` functions with plain signatures,
  so the hot handler stays small (Deegen's hot/cold split, done by hand). The later baseline JIT can
  call these same functions as its helpers.
- **CI guard.** On each toolchain bump, a test disassembles the release binary (`objdump`/
  `cargo-show-asm`) and fails if any handler contains a `call` to another handler, or ends without
  an indirect `jmp`. It also runs the three Svelte workloads to catch the Rust-1.92-style regression
  (§1.3).
- **`become` and `rust-preserve-none`.** Switch the threaded mode to them when they stabilise (2027
  at the earliest). The handler signature is already the shape they need.

### 8.3 Values

`struct Value(u64)`:

- JSC-style pointer-favouring NaN-box, with the payload being a GC pointer or a handle (§3.2);
- tags for int32, bool, null, undefined, the TDZ "hole" marker, short BigInt, string, symbol,
  object, and heap BigInt;
- f64 stored with JSC's 2^49 offset;
- `Copy`, no `Drop`, no refcount.

### 8.4 Objects, shapes, elements

- **`Shape`** (a GC object):
  - prototype pointer;
  - property count;
  - a property table shared down the transition tree (key atom → slot index plus attributes),
    searched linearly for ≤8 properties and through a lazily built hash table above that;
  - a transition table of `(atom, attrs) → weak child Shape`, inline for 1 entry and a small map
    above that;
  - an in-object slot capacity.
- **`Object` header:** `shape`, `elements` pointer, class/flags, then N in-object `Value` slots,
  then an out-of-line slots pointer for overflow.
  - For object literals, N is the literal's property count, which the compiler knows statically.
    Svelte's signals are literals (§4.4).
  - For constructors, N comes from V8-style slack tracking: start at 8, shrink after the first few
    instances (documented in V8; inferred for hocus).
- **Dictionary mode** after deleting a non-last property or above about 64 properties. It holds a
  per-object hash map and a unique shape, so ICs miss cleanly.
- **Elements kinds:** packed and holey variants of int32, f64 (raw doubles) and Value, plus a
  sparse dictionary. Transitions go one way (V8). Typed arrays use their own buffer.
- **Prototype-chain validity.** Each prototype object's shape has a validity cell, invalidated when
  that prototype or anything above it changes shape (V8's design). A prototype IC entry checks
  (receiver shape, validity cell) and loads from the holder.

### 8.5 Inline caches and the later JIT

- **Feedback vector.** One per function, allocated when the function's invocation counter plus
  back-edge counter crosses a threshold (start at 8, tune on Svelte workloads). Until then IC
  opcodes take the generic path and record nothing, so run-once code pays only the counter
  (lesson from §4.3).
- **Slot kinds:**
  - `PropertyLoad`/`PropertyStore` hold 1 entry inline, `(shape, slot index, kind:
    own-inline | own-ool | proto(holder, cell) | accessor(getter) | array-length | string-length)`,
    and then become a 4-entry polymorphic array. On overflow they mark themselves megamorphic and
    use a global stub cache keyed by `(shape, atom)`.
  - `Call` holds the last callee, or megamorphic.
  - `BinaryOp`/`Compare` hold a type-feedback bitset (int32, f64, string, other).
- **Stores that add a property** cache `(old shape → new shape, slot)`, which covers object
  construction.
- **Why this layout works for a later JIT** (inferred):
  - A Sparkplug-style baseline JIT emits, per bytecode, a guarded fast path that reads the same
    feedback slot (or embeds its current shape as a patchable immediate), and calls the same `#[cold]`
    slow-path helpers.
  - It uses the same frame layout (§8.6), so OSR and deopt are frame-compatible.
  - An optimizing tier reads the type bitsets and the call targets.

  The bytecode never changes, so a JIT can be added without touching the interpreter format.

### 8.6 Frames and the expected speed

**Frame layout.** The register file is a `Box<[Value]>` per agent. A frame is laid out as:

```
[ callee | this | new.target | argc | return ip | caller fp | feedback* | scope ]
[ parameters ... | locals and temporaries ... | outgoing arguments ... ]
```

`fp` points at the first parameter, so header fields sit at negative offsets. Operand `r` means
`fp[r]`. Outgoing arguments are the caller's top registers, and they become the callee's
parameters.

**Expected speed vs QuickJS-NG.** Speedups are on the three Svelte workloads, as multiplicative
factors, all inferred from the cited evidence:

| Contribution | signals | appmix | proxy | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| register+acc ISA (25–35% fewer dispatches) | 1.10–1.20 | 1.10–1.20 | 1.05–1.15 | §2.3 opmix; Shi et al.; Wasmi v0.32 |
| no refcounting (Copy values, tracing GC) | 1.05–1.15 | 1.05–1.15 | 1.05–1.10 | §2.2; `JS_FreeValue` 3% self plus inlined `js_dup`/free |
| cheap calls (no recursion, window args, call IC, direct builtins) | 1.15–1.30 | 1.15–1.30 | 1.05–1.15 | §5; 40/14 ops per call; 9.3 M calls |
| scope-slot closures and module slots | 1.05–1.10 | ~1.0 | 1.05–1.10 | `get_var_ref` 17–19% of dispatches |
| ICs + inline slots + elements kinds | 1.05–1.10 | 1.10–1.15 | ~1.0 | §4.4; NG PR #120 |
| tail-call threaded dispatch | 1.05–1.15 | 1.05–1.15 | 1.05–1.10 | Wasmi 1.6x for tiny wasm ops; CPython 3–5%; JS ops are heavy |
| **product (rounded)** | **1.6–2.4** | **1.5–2.2** | **1.3–1.7** | |
| **realistic v1 target** | **1.5–2.0x** | **1.3–1.7x** | **1.0–1.3x** | discounted toward V8 Ignition's measured 2.3x / 1.65x / 1.17x |

Notes on the table:

- I discount the products because V8 Ignition, with much more engineering behind it, measures
  2.3x / 1.65x / 1.17x on the same workloads (§2.4). The factors also overlap: fewer dispatches
  and no refcounts both shrink the same move opcodes.
- **wasm32** runs in portable mode without tail calls. Expect about 15–25% less (inferred from
  Wasmi's loop-match penalty, scaled down for heavier JS opcodes).
- **Risk band.** A first cut is likely 0.7–1.3x of QuickJS-NG until the fast paths are tuned.
  Every existing Rust engine sits at 0.07–0.4x (§2.4), and QuickJS's C bodies are very tight. Gate
  the port on measured parity, not on the design.

## Open questions for the user

1. **Is 1.5–2x QuickJS-NG on Svelte code, with no JIT, worth a port?** The measured ceiling for an
   interpreter is about 2x (V8 Ignition). V8 with JITs is about 19x.
   - **Recommendation:** decide on what the port buys besides speed (shared-isolate objects, a
     native svelters integration, GC). Treat speed as "at least parity, aiming at 1.5x", not as the
     reason to port.
2. **May hocus rely on unguaranteed sibling-call optimisation on stable Rust, or should it use
   nightly `become`?**
   - **Recommendation:** stable. Keep a CI assembly guard and the portable `match` fallback, and
     adopt `become` when it stabilises (planned for 2027).
3. **How much `unsafe` is allowed?**
   - **Recommendation:** a small audited core (dispatch registers, `Value` decoding, GC), behind a
     bytecode verifier, with Miri on the portable mode and fuzzing. Everything else safe.
4. **Should svelters' compiler change the Svelte proxy path?** Interpreters gain about 1.2x at best
   on `$state` proxies.
   - **Recommendation:** ask the svelters coordinator whether compiled output can avoid proxy
     traps on hot paths, for example through direct signal access for known fields. Don't expect the
     engine to fix it.
5. **Which targets must be fast?**
   - **Recommendation:** x86-64 and aarch64 (macOS, Linux, Windows) get threaded dispatch. wasm32 and
     iOS get the portable loop. Use no JIT anywhere in v1.

## First steps in this repository

1. **`bench/svelte/`.** Commit the three Svelte workloads (`entry.js` plus a reproducible rolldown
   bundle of svelte 5.57.2), the opcode-counting patch for QuickJS-NG as a `.patch` file, and a
   script that prints opcode mix and ms/10 for qjs and hocus side by side. Every later design
   choice gets measured here.
2. **Dispatch spike (1–2 weeks).**
   - About 30 opcodes: register+acc moves, int32/f64 add/compare/branch, `GetNamedProperty` with a
     monomorphic IC on a toy shape, `Call`/`Return` with window arguments.
   - Built in both threaded and portable modes from one `handlers!` macro.
   - Micro-kernels: fib, a property-read loop, closure calls, module-variable reads.
   - Compare against qjs and write up with the asm-guard test.
   - Kill criterion: worse than qjs on fib or property loops after one tuning pass.
3. **Value, Shape and feedback-vector spike** on top of step 2. Run it with the GC researcher's
   pointer-or-handle decision as a type parameter, and measure the extra cost of a handle per
   dereference on the property loop.
4. **Bytecode format document** (`docs/design/bytecode.md`). It lists opcodes, operand encoding,
   feedback slots and the verifier rules, and maps each QuickJS opcode from §2.3's top 40 to its
   hocus form, so the compiler port (from QuickJS's parser and emitter) has a target.
5. **Only then port the compiler front end** (QuickJS `js_parse_*` and emitter) to emit hocus
   bytecode. Pick the order of the remaining opcodes by the opcode-mix table.
